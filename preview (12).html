Module 5: The Role of Investment Banks in Financing

This expanded module examines how investment banks help businesses and governments raise capital, underwrite securities, execute IPOs and bond offerings, provide liquidity, value businesses, advise on mergers and acquisitions, manage transaction risk, and connect issuers with investors.

1. Investment Banks

Understanding Investment Banking

Investment banking is a specialized field of finance centered on large-scale capital raising, securities transactions, strategic advisory work, and complex corporate finance decisions. Its clients commonly include corporations, governments, institutional investors, private-equity sponsors, and other organizations that need access to substantial pools of capital or expert assistance with major transactions. Investment banks help transform a financing or strategic objective into a transaction that investors, lenders, boards, regulators, and other participants can evaluate.

The investment bank often acts as an intermediary between capital seekers and capital providers. A company may need money to expand production, purchase another business, refinance debt, enter a new market, or fund research. Investors, on the other side, are searching for opportunities that fit their required return, risk tolerance, liquidity needs, and investment mandates. Investment bankers help bridge these interests through valuation, security design, pricing, marketing, negotiation, due diligence, and transaction execution.

Investment Banking Compared with Commercial Banking

Commercial banking and investment banking both serve financial needs, but their primary activities differ. Commercial banks traditionally accept deposits, provide payment services, and make loans to households and businesses. Investment banks concentrate more heavily on capital-market transactions and strategic corporate finance. Instead of simply lending its own funds, an investment bank may help a corporation issue bonds to hundreds of institutional investors, sell new shares to the public, locate private investors, or advise management on an acquisition.

The distinction is important because the source of capital, risk allocation, and transaction process are different. A commercial loan normally creates a direct borrower-lender relationship. A securities offering may distribute financing across many investors. Investment banks therefore need expertise not only in credit but also in securities valuation, investor demand, disclosure, regulation, market conditions, negotiation, and distribution.

Two Broad Areas of Investment-Banking Activity

Investment-banking work can be viewed through two broad categories. The first is advisory activity. Advisory teams help clients evaluate mergers, acquisitions, divestitures, restructurings, recapitalizations, strategic investments, and other major decisions. Their work can include financial modeling, valuation, transaction design, negotiation support, due diligence coordination, and recommendations to boards and senior management.

The second category is capital-markets activity. Capital-markets teams help issuers raise money through equity, debt, and hybrid securities. Equity capital markets may involve IPOs, follow-on offerings, rights offerings, or private equity placements. Debt capital markets may involve investment-grade bonds, high-yield bonds, notes, or other debt securities. The investment bank evaluates market conditions, investor demand, maturity, pricing, covenants, and the timing of the transaction.

Types of Investment Banks

Investment banks vary substantially in size, geographic reach, product expertise, and target client. Large global institutions are often described as bulge-bracket banks. They typically have broad capabilities across underwriting, M&A, equity and debt capital markets, sales and trading, research, and international transactions. Their large distribution networks can be especially useful for major offerings that require access to investors in several markets.

Middle-market investment banks focus more heavily on medium-sized companies and transactions. They may provide many of the same services as larger banks but with a narrower geographic, industry, or transaction-size focus. For a middle-market company, this specialization can produce closer senior-level attention and knowledge of likely buyers, lenders, or investors.

Boutique investment banks generally specialize in a narrower set of services or industries. Some boutiques concentrate on M&A advisory, restructuring, technology, healthcare, energy, or another sector. A boutique may not provide every financing or trading service, but it can compete through specialized knowledge, experienced senior bankers, independence, and focused transaction advice.

Front Office, Middle Office, and Support Functions

Investment-banking organizations depend on several groups working together. Front-office professionals originate transactions, advise clients, execute securities offerings, conduct sales and trading, and maintain investor relationships. Risk, compliance, treasury, and control functions help evaluate exposure and ensure that transactions operate within legal and internal limits. Operations, technology, finance, and other support teams help settle trades, maintain records, protect data, and keep complex transactions functioning reliably.

An investment bank is a financial institution that helps companies, governments, and other organizations raise capital and complete complex financial transactions. It connects issuers that need money with investors that have money to invest. It may help issue stocks or bonds, advise on mergers and acquisitions, arrange private placements, provide valuation analysis, manage risk, and support the trading and distribution of securities.

Investment banks differ from commercial banks. Commercial banks primarily accept deposits and make loans, while investment banks primarily facilitate capital-market transactions and provide advisory services. A commercial bank may lend directly from its balance sheet. An investment bank may structure a bond issue, market it to investors, underwrite it, and help distribute the securities without becoming the long-term lender.

Investment banking activityBusiness value
Capital raisingTransforms a company’s financing need into securities investors can evaluate and purchase.
UnderwritingAssumes or manages offering risk and distributes securities.
AdvisoryProvides strategy, valuation, negotiation, and transaction support.

Major clients include public corporations, privately held companies, governments, institutional investors, private-equity sponsors, venture-capital funds, and financially distressed businesses. Revenue may come from underwriting spreads, advisory fees, placement fees, market-making profits, trading commissions, financing fees, and asset-management or research-related services. Reputation matters because issuers want experienced execution and investors want credible information, reliable distribution, and fair dealing.

Big idea Investment banks reduce the friction between a business seeking capital and investors seeking an opportunity. They do not eliminate business or market risk; they organize information, pricing, distribution, negotiation, and execution so that a transaction can occur more efficiently.

2. Capital Raising and Securities Issuance

Companies raise external capital when internal cash flow is insufficient for expansion, acquisitions, research, equipment, inventory, refinancing, or working capital. The company may issue equity securities, which represent ownership, or debt securities, which represent a contractual claim for repayment and interest. The choice depends on cash-flow stability, leverage, control, valuation, market conditions, and the company’s strategic objectives.

A public offering makes securities available to a broad group of investors and usually involves extensive disclosure and regulatory compliance. A private offering targets a limited group, such as institutional or accredited investors, and may be faster and more confidential, although the securities may be less liquid and subject to transfer restrictions. The primary market is where newly issued securities are sold and the issuer receives proceeds. The secondary market is where investors later trade those securities with one another; the issuer generally does not receive the trading proceeds.

DecisionEquity securitiesDebt securities
Investor claimOwnership and residual participationContractual repayment and interest claim
Cash-flow requirementNo mandatory principal repaymentInterest and principal obligations
ControlMay dilute ownership and voting powerUsually no ownership dilution
Best fitGrowth, uncertain cash flow, high investment needsPredictable cash flow and borrowing capacity

The capital-raising process normally begins with a financing objective and an analysis of how much money is needed. The bank then evaluates the issuer, recommends security type and structure, prepares disclosure and marketing materials, tests investor demand, prices the offering, allocates securities, and coordinates settlement. Good structuring matches the security with the company’s ability to pay and the investors’ desired return and risk.

3. Underwriting

Underwriting is the process through which an investment bank helps issue securities and may assume the risk of purchasing them from the issuer and reselling them to investors. The issuer receives capital under agreed terms, while investors receive securities that have been analyzed, documented, marketed, and distributed through the underwriter.

The underwriter evaluates the issuer’s financial condition, industry, management, projections, existing debt, legal risks, competitive position, and intended use of proceeds. It helps determine offering size, security type, maturity, coupon, conversion features, covenants, share count, price range, and investor targeting. Underwriters also perform due diligence and help prepare offering documents.

Underwriting risk arises when market conditions change, investor demand is weaker than expected, the issuer’s credit quality deteriorates, or the security cannot be resold at the anticipated price. The bank’s compensation must cover labor, legal and compliance work, distribution costs, capital usage, and the risk of holding inventory or unsold securities.

4. Types of Underwriting

In a firm-commitment underwriting, the investment bank purchases the entire offering from the issuer at an agreed price and then resells it to investors. The issuer receives greater certainty, but the bank bears the risk that it cannot distribute the securities at a profitable price. In a best-efforts underwriting, the bank agrees to use its best efforts to sell the offering but does not guarantee that the entire amount will be sold. The issuer bears more of the completion risk.

A standby underwriting is commonly associated with rights offerings. The underwriter agrees to purchase shares that existing shareholders do not buy. Several banks may form an underwriting syndicate to divide distribution responsibilities and risk. The lead underwriter, often called the bookrunner, coordinates the syndicate, manages the order book, advises on pricing, and oversees allocation.

Calculation: Underwriting Spread and Issuer Proceeds

A company issues 10,000,000 shares. The underwriter pays the issuer $18.40 per share and sells the shares to investors for $19.25 per share. Calculate the underwriting spread and the issuer’s gross proceeds.

Spread per share = $19.25 − $18.40 = $0.85.

Total underwriting spread = $0.85 × 10,000,000 = $8,500,000.

Issuer gross proceeds = $18.40 × 10,000,000 = $184,000,000.

Public offering value before fees = $19.25 × 10,000,000 = $192,500,000.

5. Initial Public Offerings

An initial public offering, or IPO, is the first public sale of a private company’s shares. Companies may pursue an IPO to raise growth capital, increase visibility, create a liquid market for shares, repay debt, fund acquisitions, or provide a future equity currency. The decision also brings ongoing reporting, governance, disclosure, internal-control, and public-market responsibilities.

The investment bank begins with due diligence. It examines financial statements, contracts, intellectual property, litigation, tax matters, management, customers, suppliers, market conditions, and the company’s forecasts. Lawyers, accountants, auditors, regulators, and underwriters work on offering documents that explain the company and its risks. The bank and issuer estimate valuation and establish a preliminary price range.

During the roadshow, management presents the company to institutional investors. The bookrunner records indications of interest in an order book. This book-building process helps determine demand, price sensitivity, and allocation. The final price may differ from the preliminary range. After allocation, shares begin trading in the secondary market. The first-day price may rise because the offer was underpriced, or fall because demand was overestimated or market conditions changed.

Calculation: IPO Proceeds

A company sells 12,000,000 new shares at $22 per share. The underwriting spread is 6% of gross proceeds, and legal and registration costs are $4,500,000. Calculate gross proceeds, underwriting fees, and net proceeds.

Gross IPO proceeds = 12,000,000 × $22 = $264,000,000.

Underwriting fees = 6% × $264,000,000 = $15,840,000.

Net proceeds = $264,000,000 − $15,840,000 − $4,500,000 = $243,660,000.

IPO risk An IPO does not guarantee a high valuation or a successful public market. The issuer faces market volatility, disclosure obligations, potential underpricing, dilution, lock-up restrictions, and continuing compliance costs. Investors face valuation risk, information risk, and the possibility that the share price declines after trading begins.

6. Bond and Debt Offerings

Investment banks help corporations issue bonds by analyzing credit quality, determining maturity and covenant structure, setting coupon and price, preparing offering documents, marketing the issue, and placing bonds with investors. The amount of debt should reflect the company’s investment needs and sustainable debt capacity, not simply the maximum amount investors might temporarily accept.

Bond pricing depends on market interest rates, maturity, coupon, credit quality, liquidity, security, covenants, and investor demand. Investment-grade issuers generally pay lower yields than higher-risk issuers. A company may issue bonds to finance capital projects, acquire another company, refinance existing debt, or replace expensive short-term funding with longer-term capital.

Calculation: Bond-Issuance Costs

A corporation issues $300,000,000 of bonds. The investment bank charges a 1.25% underwriting spread, and other issuance costs total $2,100,000. Calculate the underwriting cost and net proceeds.

Underwriting spread = 1.25% × $300,000,000 = $3,750,000.

Net proceeds = $300,000,000 − $3,750,000 − $2,100,000 = $294,150,000.

The company must evaluate whether the capital raised supports enough operating benefit to justify interest and issuance costs.

7. Market Makers

A market maker is a financial intermediary that continuously or regularly quotes prices at which it is willing to buy and sell a security. The bid is the price the market maker will pay to buy. The ask, or offer, is the price at which it will sell. By standing ready to trade, market makers provide liquidity and reduce the difficulty of finding a counterparty.

Market makers may buy securities into inventory when investors sell and sell from inventory when investors buy. They face inventory risk because prices can move against positions they hold. They also face volatility risk, liquidity risk, technology risk, and information risk. In less-liquid securities, spreads may be wider because the market maker needs compensation for the difficulty and risk of holding inventory.

8. Bid-Ask Spreads and Market-Maker Profits

The bid-ask spread is the difference between the ask price and bid price. It compensates the market maker for providing immediacy, processing orders, holding inventory, managing risk, and operating trading infrastructure. Highly liquid securities with many buyers and sellers often have narrow spreads. Thinly traded or volatile securities often have wider spreads.

Calculation: Bid-Ask Spread

A market maker quotes a bid of $49.80 and an ask of $50.10. Calculate the spread and the percentage spread based on the midpoint.

Spread = $50.10 − $49.80 = $0.30 per share.

Midpoint = ($49.80 + $50.10) ÷ 2 = $49.95.

Percentage spread = $0.30 ÷ $49.95 ≈ 0.601%.

Calculation: Gross Market-Maker Profit

A market maker buys 40,000 shares at the $49.80 bid and later sells them at the $50.10 ask. Ignore price movement, commissions, and inventory costs. Estimate gross spread income.

Gross profit per share = $50.10 − $49.80 = $0.30.

Gross profit = $0.30 × 40,000 = $12,000.

Actual profit may be lower if prices move, inventory is held for a long time, orders are adverse, or transaction and technology costs apply.

9. Investment Banks as Financial Advisors

Investment banks advise companies on financial strategy, capital structure, valuation, acquisitions, divestitures, restructuring, and negotiations. A bank may recommend whether a company should issue debt, equity, convertible securities, or a private placement. It may analyze market conditions, investor appetite, comparable companies, and the likely cost of capital.

Advisory work can include strategic alternatives, fairness opinions, restructuring plans, investor analysis, regulatory support, and transaction execution. A bank may prepare a pitch book describing a proposed transaction, valuation, financing structure, market data, risks, and expected benefits. The advisory relationship is distinct from underwriting: an advisor may not guarantee the purchase of securities.

10. Mergers and Acquisitions

Mergers and acquisitions, or M&A, involve combining businesses or transferring ownership of a business. On the buy side, an investment bank helps identify targets, analyze strategic fit, value the target, arrange financing, perform due diligence, negotiate terms, and close the transaction. On the sell side, it identifies potential buyers, prepares marketing materials, manages competitive bids, negotiates price and terms, and coordinates closing.

M&A advisors prepare pitch books and valuation analyses using comparable companies, precedent transactions, and discounted cash flow models. They help address purchase price, cash versus stock consideration, earnouts, representations and warranties, debt assumption, working-capital adjustments, regulatory approval, and integration planning. Advisory fees may include a retainer, milestone payments, and a success fee based on transaction value.

Calculation: Acquisition Value

A target company has EBITDA of $18,000,000. Comparable transactions support an enterprise-value-to-EBITDA multiple of 8.5×. The target has $40,000,000 of debt and $7,000,000 of cash. Estimate enterprise value and equity value.

Enterprise value = $18,000,000 × 8.5 = $153,000,000.

Equity value = Enterprise value − debt + cash.

Equity value = $153,000,000 − $40,000,000 + $7,000,000 = $120,000,000.

The transaction price may change after due diligence, negotiation, working-capital adjustments, and identification of liabilities.

11. Business Valuation in Investment Banking

Valuation estimates what a business or security may be worth under specified assumptions. Enterprise value measures the value of operations available to all capital providers. Equity value is the value attributable to common and preferred owners after considering debt, cash, and other claims.

Comparable-company analysis applies valuation multiples observed for similar public companies. Precedent-transaction analysis examines multiples paid in prior acquisitions. Discounted cash-flow analysis estimates the present value of projected future cash flows and a terminal value. Each method depends on assumptions and should be tested with sensitivity analysis.

MethodStrengthLimitation
Comparable companiesMarket-based and relatively transparentTrue comparables may be difficult to identify.
Precedent transactionsReflects actual acquisition prices and control premiumsTransactions may include unique synergies or market conditions.
DCFDirectly links value to expected cash generationHighly sensitive to forecasts, discount rate, and terminal value.

Calculation: EBITDA Multiple

A company reports EBITDA of $12,500,000. A reasonable market multiple is 7.2×. It has $30,000,000 of debt and $4,000,000 of cash. Calculate enterprise value and estimated equity value.

Enterprise value = $12,500,000 × 7.2 = $90,000,000.

Equity value = $90,000,000 − $30,000,000 + $4,000,000 = $64,000,000.

12. Agency and Placement Agents

A placement agent acts as an intermediary between a private issuer and potential investors. Unlike a firm-commitment underwriter, the agent generally does not purchase the full securities issue for resale. Instead, it identifies investors, organizes outreach, communicates the opportunity, gathers indications of interest, and helps coordinate closing.

Placement agents can help privately held companies reach institutional investors, private-equity funds, family offices, insurance companies, or other suitable sources of capital. Compensation may be a retainer, a percentage of capital raised, or both. The issuer should evaluate investor quality, disclosure standards, conflicts, and total fees. An agent does not guarantee that financing will close.

13. Private Placements

A private placement is a securities offering made to a limited group of investors rather than to the general public. Private placements may involve private equity, private debt, preferred stock, convertible securities, or mezzanine capital. They can be faster and more confidential than public offerings and may permit negotiation of customized terms.

The trade-offs include a smaller investor pool, less liquidity, transfer restrictions, complex documentation, and potentially higher return requirements from investors. Institutional and accredited investors often have greater resources to evaluate risk, but private issuers still have important disclosure and anti-fraud responsibilities. Placement agents can support investor access and transaction execution.

14. Mezzanine Financing

Mezzanine financing combines features of debt and equity. It is generally subordinate to senior bank debt, so investors demand higher returns for accepting greater risk. Mezzanine capital may include cash interest, payment-in-kind interest, warrants, conversion rights, or an equity participation feature.

Growing companies and acquisition sponsors may use mezzanine financing when senior lenders will not provide enough capital and the owners want to avoid selling a large common-equity stake. The financing can increase total leverage and create substantial cash or eventual equity obligations. It should support a business with a credible path to higher earnings, refinancing, or an exit.

15. Specialist Financing

Specialist financing is structured for situations that fall outside ordinary lending criteria. Examples include startup financing, growth-company financing, project finance, distressed-company funding, acquisition finance, royalty financing, asset-backed structures, and financing tied to a specific contract or cash flow. Investment banks identify investors whose risk tolerance and expertise match the situation.

A startup may need capital before it has revenue. A project-finance borrower may repay investors from the project’s future cash flows rather than the sponsor’s entire balance sheet. A distressed company may need new money while negotiating with creditors. Flexible structures can solve specialized problems, but complexity often means higher fees, more restrictive terms, or greater investor return requirements.

16. Investment Banking and Corporate Restructuring

Companies restructure when they face financial distress, excessive leverage, operational underperformance, strategic change, or a need to redeploy assets. Investment banks may advise on debt restructuring, recapitalization, asset sales, divestitures, spin-offs, refinancing, bankruptcy alternatives, and negotiations with creditors or investors.

A restructuring plan may exchange old debt for new debt or equity, extend maturities, reduce interest, sell noncore assets, raise new capital, or separate a division through a spin-off. The advisor analyzes liquidity, enterprise value, stakeholder priorities, and feasible strategic alternatives. Execution requires careful negotiation because shareholders, lenders, employees, suppliers, regulators, and potential buyers may have conflicting interests.

17. Investment Banking Risks

Underwriting risk: securities may not resell at the expected price.

Market risk: rates, stock prices, credit spreads, and investor sentiment may change.

Credit risk: an issuer may default or deteriorate financially.

Liquidity risk: securities may be difficult to sell without a significant price concession.

Reputation risk: failed transactions, inaccurate analysis, or misconduct can damage the bank.

Legal and regulatory risk: disclosure failures, unsuitable conduct, or violations can lead to penalties.

Conflict risk: the bank may have relationships with multiple parties whose interests differ.

Transaction risk: financing, approvals, diligence, or documentation may fail.

M&A execution risk: expected synergies may not occur and integration may damage value.

Risk-management practices include due diligence, credit limits, position limits, valuation controls, compliance review, information barriers, approval committees, stress testing, legal documentation, and monitoring of market and counterparty exposure.

18. Regulation, Ethics, and Conflicts of Interest

Information Barriers and Conflicts of Interest

Investment banks may simultaneously advise issuers, serve investors, publish research, trade securities, and finance transactions. These activities can create conflicts if confidential information obtained in one role influences another. Firms use information barriers, restricted lists, surveillance, disclosure procedures, and separate reporting lines to reduce inappropriate information flow.

Client Suitability, Fair Dealing, and Reputation

A transaction can be legally executable yet still create reputational or client-relationship risk if the structure is unsuitable, disclosure is weak, or incentives are poorly aligned. Investment banks depend heavily on credibility. Issuers expect accurate advice and reliable execution, while investors expect fair access to material information and honest presentation of risks. Long-term reputation can therefore be more valuable than the fee from any single transaction.

Securities regulation seeks to promote fair markets, reliable disclosure, and investor protection. Investment banks must address registration and disclosure requirements, anti-fraud standards, suitability and conduct obligations where applicable, insider-trading restrictions, recordkeeping, and conflicts of interest. Material nonpublic information must be controlled through information barriers and restricted-list procedures.

Ethical conduct requires accurate presentation of risks, fair allocation of securities, honest communication about valuation, and transparent disclosure of compensation and conflicts. A bank may advise a company, underwrite its securities, make a market, and serve other clients in the same industry. These relationships require policies and supervision designed to prevent confidential information or conflicting incentives from unfairly influencing the transaction.

19. Sales and Trading

Sales and trading activities connect institutional investors with financial markets. Sales professionals maintain relationships with asset managers, pension funds, insurance companies, hedge funds, corporations, and other investors. Traders execute transactions in equities, bonds, currencies, commodities, and derivatives. These activities can support an investment bank's underwriting and market-making functions because successful securities issuance depends on understanding investor demand and market liquidity.

Trading activity is not risk free. A bank may hold securities temporarily, facilitate a client order, hedge an exposure, or quote prices to provide liquidity. Market prices can change before the bank offsets a position. Effective controls therefore include position limits, hedging policies, valuation procedures, liquidity monitoring, and independent risk oversight. The economic objective is not merely to trade frequently; it is to provide client execution and market access while controlling inventory and market risk.

Application: Trading and Liquidity

Suppose an institutional client needs to sell a large bond position. An investment bank may locate buyers, purchase part of the position into inventory, or break the order into smaller transactions. The bank's distribution network and market knowledge can reduce the difficulty of executing the sale, but the bank may accept temporary price risk. This example illustrates why liquidity provision and risk management are closely connected.

20. Research and Analysis

Research supports investment decisions by analyzing companies, industries, securities, and economic conditions. Equity research may examine revenue growth, margins, competitive position, valuation multiples, earnings forecasts, and industry trends. Fixed-income research may focus on leverage, cash flow, credit quality, covenant protection, interest-rate exposure, and the probability of repayment.

Research can also contribute to capital raising by helping bankers understand how investors may view an issuer. However, research must remain subject to appropriate independence and conflict controls. An investment bank that is simultaneously seeking underwriting business from a company must avoid presenting promotional analysis as objective research. Information barriers, disclosure requirements, supervisory review, and analyst-independence rules are therefore important parts of a credible investment-banking organization.

21. Risk-Management Advisory

Investment banks may help clients identify and manage exposures to interest rates, foreign exchange rates, commodity prices, and other financial risks. A company with variable-rate debt may be concerned that rising rates will increase interest expense. An exporter may be concerned that currency movements will reduce the domestic value of foreign sales. A manufacturer may face volatility in energy or raw-material prices.

Risk-management advisory begins by identifying the underlying business exposure. The bank can then evaluate instruments such as forwards, futures, options, or swaps. The objective is normally to reduce unwanted volatility rather than to speculate. A hedge should be matched to the amount, timing, and nature of the exposure. Management must also consider counterparty risk, collateral requirements, accounting treatment, liquidity, termination costs, and the possibility that a hedge performs differently from the underlying exposure.

Application: Interest-Rate Exposure

Assume a company expects to issue long-term debt in six months but fears that market rates may rise before the financing is completed. An investment bank can help management analyze hedging alternatives designed to reduce the risk of a higher future borrowing cost. The decision should compare the cost of the hedge with the potential financial effect of adverse rate movements and should remain consistent with the company's risk policy.

22. Asset Management and Investment Solutions

Some large financial institutions that conduct investment-banking activities also operate asset-management businesses. 23. Equity Capital Markets and Debt Capital Markets

Equity Capital Markets, commonly abbreviated ECM, focuses on transactions involving ownership securities. ECM professionals analyze valuation, investor demand, dilution, market timing, transaction size, and the appropriate structure for IPOs and follow-on equity offerings. They work closely with industry bankers, syndicate desks, lawyers, accountants, management, and institutional investors.

Debt Capital Markets, or DCM, focuses on bond and other debt issuance. DCM professionals evaluate interest rates, credit spreads, maturity, coupon structure, covenants, investor demand, refinancing needs, and the issuer's existing leverage. The objective is to structure debt that raises the required capital while maintaining a sustainable repayment profile and acceptable financing cost.

ECM versus DCM Decision

A company with rapid growth but uncertain near-term cash flow may prefer equity because equity does not create mandatory principal and interest payments. A mature company with stable cash flow may prefer debt when it wants to avoid ownership dilution. Investment bankers model both alternatives and may recommend a combination when balancing leverage, control, cost, flexibility, and market capacity.

24. Investment Banks and the Financial System

Investment banks contribute to capital formation by helping transfer funds from investors to businesses and governments that can use those funds for productive activity. When a company issues shares to finance a new factory or a government issues bonds for infrastructure, the capital-market process can direct savings toward long-term investment. Investment banks assist with the structure, pricing, disclosure, marketing, and execution needed to complete these transactions.

They also support market liquidity through trading and market-making activity. Liquidity matters because investors are more willing to purchase securities when they expect to be able to sell them later at a reasonably observable market price. Better liquidity can improve price discovery and can reduce the liquidity premium investors demand, although liquidity can decline rapidly during periods of stress.

Investment banks also contribute to market efficiency by producing valuation work, gathering investor feedback, conducting due diligence, and helping establish prices for new securities. These activities do not guarantee that prices are always correct. Instead, they improve the flow of information and create mechanisms through which issuers and investors can negotiate and transact.

25. Cross-Border Financing and Global Capital Flows

Large companies and governments may seek capital outside their home markets. 26. The Investment-Banking Transaction Process

Although transactions differ, many investment-banking engagements follow a common sequence. First, management identifies a strategic or financing objective. Second, the bank analyzes the company's financial condition, valuation, capital structure, industry, and alternatives. Third, the bank and client select a transaction structure and prepare financial models, presentation materials, disclosures, and supporting documentation.

The next stage involves due diligence and market preparation. For a securities offering, the bank may test investor interest, organize management presentations, build an order book, and recommend final pricing. For an acquisition, the process may involve identifying counterparties, evaluating bids, coordinating due diligence, negotiating terms, and arranging financing. After signing or pricing, the bank helps manage closing requirements and settlement.

Post-transaction responsibilities also matter. A newly public company must satisfy continuing reporting and governance requirements. A borrower must comply with debt terms. An acquiring company must integrate the target. Investment banking therefore should be evaluated not only by whether a transaction closes but also by whether the financing or strategic structure remains workable afterward.

27. Investment-Banking Revenue Model

Investment banks can earn revenue in several ways. Underwriting revenue commonly comes from the difference between the amount investors pay and the amount the issuer receives, subject to the offering structure. Advisory revenue may include retainers, milestone payments, and success fees. Placement agents may receive fees based on capital raised. Market makers may earn bid-ask spreads, while trading businesses can earn or lose money from execution and inventory positions.

Revenue should be evaluated together with risk. A large underwriting fee may compensate for substantial capital commitment and distribution risk. A success fee can motivate transaction execution but can also create a conflict if the advisor benefits more from closing a transaction than from recommending that the client walk away. Strong governance requires clear engagement terms, conflict disclosure, independent board judgment, and appropriate compliance review.

28. Investment Banking and Economic Development

32. Expanded Module Review: How the Functions Fit Together

The major functions of investment banking are interconnected. Capital raising requires valuation, due diligence, investor communication, pricing, underwriting or placement, and distribution. M&A advisory requires valuation, negotiation, financing analysis, risk assessment, and execution. Market making supports liquidity after securities are issued. For a business manager, the central lesson is that an investment bank is not simply a source of money. It is a financial intermediary and transaction specialist that helps a client design, price, market, negotiate, finance, and execute major strategic decisions. 34. Final Summary

Investment banks occupy several connected roles in the financing system. First, they help businesses raise debt and equity capital by designing securities, analyzing investor demand, preparing disclosures, and distributing new issues. Second, through underwriting, they may assume offering risk or use best efforts to place securities. Third, as market makers, they provide bid and ask quotations that make secondary-market trading more liquid and efficient.

Fourth, investment banks act as financial advisors. They advise on capital structure, valuation, acquisitions, divestitures, restructurings, recapitalizations, and strategic alternatives. Their M&A work includes identifying targets or buyers, performing valuation and due diligence, negotiating terms, arranging financing, and coordinating closing. Fifth, they serve as agents and placement intermediaries for private companies that need access to institutional or specialized investors without conducting a broad public offering.

The expanded activities in this module show how these roles connect. An IPO combines advisory work, valuation, due diligence, underwriting, investor marketing, allocation, and eventually market making. A bond issue combines credit analysis, security structuring, pricing, underwriting, and placement. A private acquisition may use valuation, M&A advice, mezzanine financing, private placement, and restructuring expertise. A distressed company may require debt negotiations, asset sales, new capital, and specialist investors.

Investment banking creates access and efficiency, but it does not eliminate risk. Issuers still face market conditions, dilution, interest obligations, disclosure requirements, execution challenges, and transaction costs. Investors still face credit, liquidity, valuation, and market risk. The best use of an investment bank occurs when its network, expertise, reputation, and execution capabilities add more value than the fees and complexity it introduces.

Problem 22 — Market-Maker Inventory Loss — Franklin Securities

Case

Franklin Securities buys 100,000 shares at $40.00 expecting to sell near $40.20. Before it sells, market price falls to $39.50 and Franklin exits at that price.

Required

Calculate expected spread income if sold at $40.20.

Calculate actual trading loss relative to purchase cost when sold at $39.50.

Explain inventory risk.

Complete Solution

Solution

Expected spread income = ($40.20 - $40.00) × 100,000 = $20,000. Actual loss = ($39.50 - $40.00) × 100,000 = -$50,000.

Interpretation

The $50,000 price loss overwhelms the expected $20,000 spread. Market makers earn spreads but bear the risk that inventory prices move adversely before positions are offset.

Problem 25 — CPA-Style Integrated Investment-Banking Case — Thompson Medical Group

Case

Thompson Medical Group, led by CEO Carla Thompson and CFO Daniel Price, is considering an IPO and acquisition. The IPO would issue 15,000,000 new shares at $28. The underwriter buys them from Thompson at $26.60. Other IPO costs are $8,000,000. Thompson plans to acquire Precision Labs, which has EBITDA of $18,000,000. Comparable transactions support 8.5× EBITDA. Precision has $35,000,000 debt and $7,000,000 cash. Thompson expects to use IPO net proceeds toward the acquisition.

Required

Calculate public IPO value.

Calculate underwriting spread per share and total spread.

Calculate issuer proceeds before other costs and net IPO proceeds after other costs.

Calculate Precision's enterprise value.

Calculate Precision's estimated equity value.

Compare IPO net proceeds with estimated equity purchase price and calculate the difference.

Discuss at least six execution risks or advisory issues.

Complete Solution

IPO calculations

Public offering value = 15,000,000 × $28 = $420,000,000. Spread per share = $28 - $26.60 = $1.40. Total underwriting spread = $21,000,000. Issuer proceeds before other costs = 15,000,000 × $26.60 = $399,000,000. Net proceeds = $399,000,000 - $8,000,000 = $391,000,000.

Discussion 1 — Investment Banks and Capital Raising

Discussion Question

How do investment banks help companies raise capital through stocks and bonds, and what advantages and limitations should management consider when using an investment bank?

Discussion Response

Investment banks are most useful when a financing decision involves more than simply finding someone willing to provide money. A company preparing to issue shares or bonds has to decide how much capital it needs, what type of security fits its cash flow, how investors are likely to value the offering, and whether market conditions are favorable. The investment bank helps organize those decisions and turns the company's financing need into a transaction that investors can evaluate.

For an equity offering, the bank may help management estimate valuation, decide how many shares to issue, prepare offering materials, market the company to investors, and build an order book. For a bond issue, the work shifts toward credit quality, maturity, coupon structure, covenants, market yields, and debt capacity. In both cases, the bank also helps the issuer understand what the market may accept rather than relying only on management's preferred price.

The advantage is access and execution. An established investment bank already has relationships with institutional investors and understands how similar securities are being priced. That network can make a large offering more practical than a company trying to contact investors individually. The bank can also coordinate lawyers, accountants, regulators, roadshows, due diligence, pricing, allocation, and closing so that the financing process moves as one transaction instead of many disconnected tasks.

Using an investment bank does not guarantee that the company will receive the highest possible valuation or that investors will buy the securities at any price. Market conditions can change quickly, and an issuer may have to reduce the offering price, postpone the deal, or accept a different financing structure. Fees and underwriting spreads also reduce the amount of capital the company actually keeps.

For that reason, I would view an investment bank as a financial intermediary and transaction specialist rather than simply a source of funds. Its value comes from helping the company select, structure, price, market, and execute a financing method that matches the company's needs and the market's willingness to supply capital.

Discussion 2 — Underwriting and Risk

Discussion Question

Why is underwriting important when a company issues securities? Compare firm-commitment and best-efforts underwriting and discuss how each arrangement distributes risk.

Discussion Response

Underwriting matters because an issuer wants confidence that a securities offering will actually produce the capital needed. The investment bank evaluates the issuer, helps set the offering price, markets the securities, and determines how much distribution risk it is willing to accept. The exact risk depends on the underwriting agreement.

In a firm-commitment underwriting, the investment bank agrees to purchase the securities from the issuer and resell them to investors. The issuer receives the agreed amount from the underwriter, while the bank accepts the risk that it may have to sell the securities for less than expected. The underwriting spread compensates the bank for work such as due diligence, marketing, distribution, capital commitment, and the possibility of unfavorable market movement.

A best-efforts arrangement places more of the financing risk on the issuer. The bank agrees to use its distribution network and marketing ability to sell the securities, but it does not guarantee that the entire issue will be sold. If investor demand is weak, the company may raise less money than planned. That can be a serious problem if the issuer has already committed to an acquisition, construction project, or debt repayment that depends on the offering.

The pricing decision creates another trade-off. Setting the price too high may reduce investor demand and leave securities unsold. Setting it too low can make distribution easier but may cause the issuer to give up value. An IPO that rises sharply on the first trading day can benefit new investors, but management may question whether the company could have raised more capital by setting a higher offer price.

The strongest underwriting decision therefore balances certainty, cost, pricing, and risk. Management should understand exactly what the bank is committing to purchase, what fees will be paid, what happens if markets deteriorate, and how much net cash the company will receive after the spread and other offering expenses.

Discussion 3 — Mergers, Acquisitions, and Advisory Services

Discussion Question

Suppose a company is considering acquiring a competitor. What services can an investment bank provide, and how can valuation, due diligence, negotiation, and financing affect the transaction?

Discussion Response

An acquisition can look attractive because it promises growth, new customers, technology, geographic expansion, or cost savings, but the purchase price still has to make financial sense. An investment bank can help the buyer move from a strategic idea to a transaction by identifying targets, valuing the business, reviewing financing alternatives, coordinating due diligence, and assisting with negotiations.

Valuation is one of the most important parts of the process. A banker may use comparable-company multiples, precedent transactions, and discounted cash flow analysis rather than relying on one number. If a target produces $18 million of EBITDA and comparable transactions suggest an 8.5 times multiple, that information can provide a starting point for enterprise value. Debt, cash, working-capital adjustments, and other obligations then affect the amount ultimately attributable to shareholders.

Due diligence tests whether the assumptions behind the valuation are realistic. The buyer needs to understand customer concentration, contracts, debt, taxes, litigation, employee obligations, technology, intellectual property, and the quality of reported earnings. A company that appears inexpensive can become costly if due diligence uncovers liabilities or shows that expected cash flows are weaker than management believed.

Financing also affects whether the deal creates value. Paying with cash can reduce liquidity. Borrowing can increase leverage and interest expense. Issuing shares can dilute existing owners. The investment bank can model these choices and estimate how the acquisition may affect debt ratios, interest coverage, ownership, and earnings per share. Accretion by itself does not prove that a deal is good, just as dilution does not automatically prove that it is bad.

I would judge an acquisition by whether the strategic benefits and realistic future cash flows justify the total price and financing risk. The bank's role is valuable because it combines valuation, financing, negotiation, market knowledge, and execution, but the board and management still have to decide whether the transaction creates enough long-term value to justify the risks.

Discussion 4 — Market Making and Liquidity

Discussion Question

How do market makers contribute to financial-market liquidity, and how does the bid-ask spread create both a revenue opportunity and risk for the dealer?

Discussion Response

Market makers help financial markets function by standing ready to buy and sell securities. The bid is the price at which the market maker is willing to buy, while the ask is the price at which it is willing to sell. The difference between those prices is the bid-ask spread. By quoting both sides of the market, the dealer makes it easier for investors to trade without waiting for another investor with the exact opposite order.

Liquidity matters to both investors and issuers. Investors generally prefer securities that can be sold without a large price concession. If a security is difficult to trade, investors may demand a higher return to compensate for that inconvenience and risk. A more liquid secondary market can therefore make a security more attractive, even though the issuer normally does not receive cash when investors trade with one another after the original offering.

The spread is not guaranteed profit. Suppose a dealer buys 40,000 shares at a $49.80 bid and later sells all of them at a $50.10 ask. If nothing else changes, the gross spread income is $12,000. In practice, the market price may fall while the shares are held. The dealer may have to sell below the expected ask, hedge the position, finance inventory, and pay technology, exchange, personnel, and compliance costs.

Large orders create an additional challenge. If an institutional investor wants to sell a position that is much larger than normal trading volume, immediately placing the entire order into the market may push the price down. An investment bank may locate buyers, purchase part of the position into inventory, or execute the trade gradually. Each choice involves a balance between speed, price impact, and inventory exposure.

Market making therefore connects liquidity with risk management. The dealer earns compensation for providing immediacy, but it must control position size, monitor market movement, hedge when appropriate, and avoid holding more inventory than it can reasonably manage. The service is valuable because it helps keep capital markets usable after securities have been issued.

Discussion 5 — Ethics and Conflicts of Interest

Discussion Question

Investment banks may provide underwriting, advisory, research, trading, and other services at the same time. What conflicts can arise, and what controls help protect clients and investors?

Discussion Response

Investment banks can face conflicts because the same financial institution may advise a company, underwrite its securities, trade those securities, publish research, lend money, and serve investors at the same time. Those activities are not automatically improper, but the interests of the different clients are not always the same. A bank has to recognize where incentives conflict and create controls before confidential information or fee pressure affects a decision.

One example involves research and underwriting. A company seeking an IPO wants a strong valuation and favorable investor interest. Investors, however, need analysis that presents risks as well as opportunities. If research is influenced by the desire to win underwriting fees, investors may receive an overly positive picture. Information barriers, separate reporting lines, supervisory review, disclosure, and analyst-independence procedures help reduce that risk.

M&A advisory can create another conflict. An advisor may earn a large success fee if a transaction closes. That fee structure can create an incentive to favor completion even when new information makes the deal less attractive. Management and the board should understand the advisor's compensation, evaluate the analysis independently, and remain willing to stop or renegotiate a transaction when the economics no longer support it.

Confidential information also requires careful treatment. Bankers may learn about an acquisition, earnings problem, restructuring, or securities offering before the public knows about it. That information cannot be used casually by trading personnel or passed to favored clients. Restricted lists, surveillance, information barriers, access controls, and compliance review are important because a bank's reputation depends on clients trusting it with sensitive information.

Ethics in investment banking is therefore more than following a rule after a problem occurs. It requires accurate disclosure, fair dealing, careful handling of confidential information, transparent compensation, and a willingness to present risks even when doing so may reduce a fee or make a transaction harder to complete. A bank that loses credibility with issuers, investors, regulators, or counterparties can damage a relationship that is worth far more than one transaction.
